01 · Overview

What Is the Solar System?

The Solar System consists of the Sun and the celestial objects that orbit it, including planets, dwarf planets, moons, asteroids, comets, and smaller bodies.

The Sun is at the center of the Solar System and contains more than 99% of its total mass. Its gravity helps keep objects in orbit.

Remember: The eight planets in order from the Sun are Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars, Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune.
02 · Vocabulary

Essential Terms

Gravity

The force of attraction between bodies due to their masses.

Nebula

A cloud of gas and dust in interstellar space.

Nebular Hypothesis

The idea that the Solar System formed from a rotating cloud of gas and dust that flattened into a disk.

Orbit

The path followed by a celestial body as it moves around another object.

Satellite

A body that orbits a planet or another celestial body.

Terrestrial Planets

The rocky inner planets: Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars.

Jovian Planets

The giant outer planets: Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune.

03 · Small Solar System Bodies

Comets

A comet is a small celestial object that orbits the Sun. It contains ice, dust, rock, and frozen gases. Comets are sometimes called dirty snowballs.

Nucleus

The solid central part of a comet, containing frozen materials and dust.

Coma

A cloud of gas and dust that forms around the nucleus when the comet is heated by the Sun.

Tail

Gas and dust released from the comet. The tail generally points away from the Sun due to the solar wind and sunlight.

Oort Cloud

A distant region thought to contain many icy bodies, including possible sources of long-period comets.

As a comet approaches the Sun:
  1. Its ice heats up and releases gas and dust.
  2. A coma forms around the nucleus.
  3. Gas and dust form tails that point generally away from the Sun.

Halley's Comet: It was last seen from Earth in 1986 and takes about 76 years to orbit the Sun.

04 · Asteroids

Asteroids and the Asteroid Belt

An asteroid is a small, generally rocky, solid body that orbits the Sun. Asteroids range from tiny objects to bodies hundreds of kilometers across.

Asteroid Belt

The region between the orbits of Mars and Jupiter where many asteroids are found.

Ceres was once considered the largest asteroid. In 2006, it was classified as a dwarf planet.

The presentation lists Vesta, Pallas, and Hygiea among the largest asteroids, with approximate diameters of 530 km, 522 km, and 430 km, respectively.

Do not confuse them: Asteroids are generally rocky, while comets contain significant amounts of ice and can develop a coma and tail near the Sun.
05 · Meteoroids, Meteors, and Meteorites

Three Related Terms

TermMeaningWhere?
MeteoroidA small solid body moving through space.Space
MeteorThe bright streak of light produced when a meteoroid enters Earth's atmosphere and heats up.Atmosphere
MeteoriteA fragment that survives its passage through the atmosphere and reaches the ground.Ground
Memory trick: A meteoroid is in space, a meteor glows in the atmosphere, and a meteorite reaches the ground.

How a Meteor Forms

  1. A meteoroid travels through space.
  2. It enters Earth's atmosphere.
  3. Interactions with the atmosphere cause intense heating.
  4. The object and surrounding gas glow, creating a streak of light.
  5. If a fragment survives and reaches the ground, it is called a meteorite.

Types of Meteoroids by Composition

Stony

Made mainly of rocky minerals.

Iron

Made mainly of iron and nickel; typically dense.

Stony-Iron

Contain both metal and rocky materials and are less common.

Where Do Meteoroids Come From?

  • Comets: Release dust and rocky fragments as they travel near the Sun.
  • Asteroids: Collisions can break off pieces.
  • Space-rock collisions: Impacts produce smaller fragments.

Scientists study meteoroids to learn about the early Solar System, the composition of space objects, and objects that may approach Earth.

06 · Dwarf Planets

What Is a Dwarf Planet?

A dwarf planet orbits the Sun and is nearly round because of its own gravity, but it has not cleared other objects from its orbital neighborhood. It is not a moon.

IAU Classification Criteria

  • It orbits the Sun.
  • It is nearly round due to its own gravity.
  • It has not cleared its orbital neighborhood.
  • It is not a satellite of another body.

Pluto

Located in the Kuiper Belt; reclassified as a dwarf planet in 2006.

Eris

A distant dwarf planet that is slightly smaller than Pluto in diameter.

Haumea

Known for its elongated shape and rapid rotation.

Makemake

Located in the Kuiper Belt and has a very cold surface.

Ceres

Located in the asteroid belt; formerly classified as an asteroid.

Why is Pluto a dwarf planet? Pluto orbits the Sun, is nearly round, and is not a moon, but it has not cleared other objects from its orbital region.
07 · Quick Review

Remember These Facts

  • The Sun contains more than 99% of the Solar System's total mass.
  • Terrestrial planets: Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars.
  • Jovian planets: Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, and Neptune.
  • Comets contain ice, dust, rock, and frozen gases.
  • The asteroid belt lies between Mars and Jupiter.
  • A meteoroid is in space; a meteor is the streak of light; a meteorite reaches the ground.
  • Pluto was reclassified as a dwarf planet in 2006.
  • Halley's Comet has an orbital period of about 76 years.
