What Are Colloids?

Start with the particles that make up a colloidal system.

A colloid is a mixture in which very small particles are dispersed throughout another substance. The particles are larger than those in a true solution but generally small enough that they do not settle out like particles in a suspension.

Dispersed particles

Like the solute

The substance distributed throughout the mixture. It is the dispersed component.

Dispersing medium

Like the solvent

The substance in which the dispersed particles are distributed.

Compare solutions, colloids, and suspensions

Remember: Ordinary filter paper does not separate colloidal particles from the dispersing medium, but it can retain many suspension particles.

Gas particles are spread through a liquid. Examples include whipped cream and beaten egg white.

Gel

A semisolid colloid with relatively high viscosity. Examples include cheese, butter, and margarine.

Solid particles are dispersed through a liquid, as in paint or a starch mixture.

Classification tip: Do not classify a colloid by its appearance alone. Identify the state of the dispersed particles first, then the state of the medium.

Properties of Colloids

Four important properties of colloids.

Property 1

Tyndall Effect

The Tyndall effect is the scattering of light by colloidal particles, making the path of a light beam visible.

John Tyndall, a British physicist, investigated this effect.

Property 2

Brownian Movement

Brownian movement is the rapid, random motion of dispersed particles through the dispersing medium.

Robert Brown, a Scottish botanist, observed this movement in 1827.

Property 3

Adsorptive Property

Adsorption is the ability of a substance to physically hold another substance on its surface. The key idea is that the substance is held at the surface rather than being described as spread throughout the whole material.

Property 4

Electrical Charge

Colloidal particles adsorb ions present in the dispersing medium. Depending on the colloid, the particles attract either positive ions or negative ions, rather than both types.

Preparation of Colloids

The lesson describes two general methods.

Method 1

Dispersion

Dispersion breaks down larger particles until they reach colloidal size.

Methods listed: grinding, using an emulsifying agent, homogenization, and peptization.

Method 2

Condensation

Condensation joins particles of molecular size to form particles of colloidal size. Double decomposition reactions and physical methods can also be used.

Important terms

Emulsifying agent

A substance that stabilizes an emulsion so it does not separate into its components.

Homogenization

The process of blending or emulsifying a substance by forcing it through fine openings against a hard surface.

Peptizing agent

The lesson describes a peptizing agent as an enzyme in the body that breaks down food particles to colloidal size.

Examples of condensation reactions

FeCl₃ + 3H₂O → Fe(OH)₃ + 3HCl

Ferric chloride + water → ferric hydroxide + hydrochloric acid

Al₂(SO₄)₃ + 6H₂O → 2Al(OH)₃ + 3H₂SO₄
Distinguish the methods: Dispersion breaks larger particles down. Condensation brings smaller molecular particles together to form colloidal-sized particles.
5

Dialysis and Hemodialysis

How a membrane can separate small particles from colloidal material.

Colloidal-sized substances, such as blood proteins, are retained by a membrane, while smaller molecules and ions can pass through. Dialysis is the selective passage of small particles through a dialyzing membrane.

Hemodialysis = dialysis of blood using a kidney machine
The central idea is selective passage through a membrane: smaller particles can pass, while colloidal-sized substances are retained.
6

Classification by Attraction to the Medium

Lyophilic and lyophobic sols differ in their attraction to the medium.

Lyophilic

Solvent-loving

The dispersed phase has a strong affinity for the medium. The particles attach themselves to solvent particles and can form complex ions or molecules.

If water is the medium, these sols are called hydrophilic.

Examples: glue, gelatin, albumin.

Lyophobic

Solvent-fearing

The dispersed particles do not form complex ions or molecules with the solvent particles.

Memory aid: “Lyophilic” means solvent-loving, while “lyophobic” means solvent-fearing. Focus on the attraction between the dispersed particles and the medium.
7

Gels, Emulsions, Foams, and Aerosols

Special colloidal systems and what helps keep them stable.

Gels and syneresis

Gels are semisolids with high viscosity. One explanation for their rigidity is that colloidal particles arrange as fibrils.

Syneresis is the separation of liquid from a gel due to contraction. An example is serum oozing from a blood clot.

Emulsions

An emulsion is a mixture of one liquid in another. Emulsions can be stabilized by a protective colloid, such as egg yolk in mayonnaise. An emulsion is stable if it does not separate on standing.

Foams

Foams consist of gas dispersed in a very viscous liquid. Protein solutions can produce stable foams, such as meringue made with egg albumin and marshmallow. Hop helps stabilize beer foam.

Aerosols

Aerosols are colloidal dispersions of liquid in a gas or solid in a gas. Examples include fog, mist, smoke, and dust in air.

8

Protective Colloids

Colloids that help keep dispersed particles from separating.

A protective colloid is a lyophilic colloid added to a lyophobic sol to reduce its sensitivity to precipitation caused by an electrolyte. In simpler terms, protective colloids help prevent dispersed particles from separating in an unstable colloidal system.

Protective colloidUse
GelatinAdded to ice cream to help prevent ice crystal formation and give it a smooth texture.
GlueAdded to foamite fire extinguisher to keep carbon dioxide bubbles in the colloidal state.
Gum arabicKeeps carbon particles dispersed in water in India ink.
TanninKeeps graphite dispersed in oil or water for use as a lubricant.
Egg yolkStabilizes mayonnaise.
Key idea: Protective colloids improve stability by helping the dispersed particles remain distributed instead of separating out.

Quick Review and Practice

Try to answer before revealing each explanation.

1. What are the two components of a colloid?

Answer: Dispersed particles and the dispersing medium.

2. What particle-size range does the lesson give for colloids?

Answer: 1 to 100 nm.

3. What is the Tyndall effect?

Answer: The scattering of light by colloidal particles, which makes the beam visible.

4. What is Brownian movement?

Answer: The rapid, random motion of dispersed particles through the dispersing medium.

5. What is the difference between dispersion and condensation?

Answer: Dispersion breaks larger particles down to colloidal size; condensation joins molecular-sized particles to form colloidal-sized particles.

6. What type of colloid is liquid dispersed in gas?

Answer: Liquid aerosol, such as fog or mist.

7. What is syneresis?

Answer: The separation of liquid from a gel caused by contraction.

8. Define dialysis.

Answer: Selective passage of small particles through a dialyzing membrane.

9. What is the difference between lyophilic and lyophobic sols?

Answer: Lyophilic sols have strong affinity between the dispersed phase and medium; lyophobic particles do not form complex ions or molecules with the solvent particles.

10. Give two protective colloids and their uses.

Answer: Examples include gelatin in ice cream and egg yolk in mayonnaise. Gum arabic also keeps carbon particles dispersed in India ink.
