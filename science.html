Fossils and Relative and Absolute Dating

Learn how scientists use fossils, rock-layer principles, and simplified half-life evidence to determine the age of rocks and fossils.

1. What Is Dating in Geology?

In geology, dating means determining the age of rocks, fossils, or other geological materials.

Scientists use evidence from rocks and fossils to understand when geological events happened and how Earth's history developed.

Relative Dating

Determines whether something is older or younger than something else without giving an exact numerical age.

Absolute / Numerical Dating

Determines an approximate numerical age, usually using radioactive decay and half-life.

2. Fossils

A fossil is the preserved remains, impression, or evidence of an organism that lived in the past.

Fossils are commonly found in sedimentary rocks. They can provide important information about organisms that lived long ago and the environments in which they lived.

Why Are Fossils Useful?

  • They provide evidence of ancient life.
  • They help scientists compare the ages of rock layers.
  • They can help identify environments that existed in the past.
  • Certain fossils can be used as indicators of geological age.
Key idea: Fossils are not just preserved organisms. They are also evidence that helps scientists reconstruct Earth's history.

3. Relative Dating

Relative dating places rocks, fossils, or geological events in chronological order.

It tells us which material is older or younger, but it does not necessarily tell us the exact number of years ago it formed.

Example

If Rock Layer A is below Rock Layer B, and the layers have not been disturbed, Layer A is generally older than Layer B.

4. Principle of Superposition

The principle of superposition states that in an undisturbed sequence of sedimentary rock layers, the oldest layer is generally at the bottom and the youngest layer is at the top.

Remember: Bottom = generally older
Top = generally younger

Example

Imagine four undisturbed rock layers:

  1. Layer D at the top
  2. Layer C
  3. Layer B
  4. Layer A at the bottom

The relative order from oldest to youngest is: A → B → C → D.

5. Other Principles Used in Relative Dating

Original Horizontality

Sedimentary layers are generally deposited in approximately horizontal layers. If layers are tilted or folded, a geological event likely affected them after deposition.

Cross-Cutting Relationships

A geological feature that cuts through another rock or feature is generally younger than what it cuts.

Example: If a crack or igneous intrusion cuts through several rock layers, the cutting feature formed after those layers.

Fossil Succession

Fossil organisms appear and disappear from the geological record in a recognizable sequence. Scientists can use these fossil patterns to compare the relative ages of rock layers.

6. Index Fossils

An index fossil is a fossil that is useful for identifying and comparing the relative ages of rock layers.

Good index fossils generally come from organisms that were widespread but existed for a relatively short period of geological time.

Why Are They Useful?

  • They can help match rock layers from different locations.
  • They help determine relative age.
  • They provide clues about when a rock layer formed.
Important: An index fossil is mainly used for comparing relative ages. It does not automatically give an exact numerical age.

7. Absolute or Numerical Dating

Absolute dating, also called numerical dating, attempts to determine an age in years or another numerical unit.

One important method uses radioactive isotopes and their predictable rates of decay.

Radioactive Decay

Some unstable atoms naturally change into more stable forms over time. This process is called radioactive decay.

Scientists can use the amount of radioactive material remaining and the products of decay to estimate the age of certain materials.

8. Half-Life

A half-life is the amount of time required for half of a radioactive sample to decay.

After every half-life, the amount of the original radioactive material is reduced by half.

After 1 half-life → 1/2 remains
After 2 half-lives → 1/4 remains
After 3 half-lives → 1/8 remains
After 4 half-lives → 1/16 remains
Example: Suppose a sample starts with 100 units of radioactive material and one half-life has passed.

100 ÷ 2 = 50 units remain.

After another half-life:
50 ÷ 2 = 25 units remain.
Memory trick: Half-life means the sample becomes half of its previous amount, not zero.

9. Simplified Half-Life Evidence

Half-Lives Passed Fraction Remaining Percentage Remaining
0 1 100%
1 1/2 50%
2 1/4 25%
3 1/8 12.5%
4 1/16 6.25%
Quick example: If 25% of the original radioactive material remains, approximately 2 half-lives have passed.

10. Relative vs. Absolute Dating

Relative Dating Absolute / Numerical Dating
Determines older vs. younger. Estimates a numerical age.
Uses rock-layer relationships and fossils. Can use radioactive decay and half-life.
Does not necessarily give an exact number of years. Provides an age expressed numerically.
Useful for establishing chronological order. Useful for estimating when something formed or lived.
Easy way to remember: Relative = which came first?
Numerical = about how old is it?

11. How Scientists Use Both Methods

Relative and numerical dating can complement each other.

Scientists may first use rock layers and fossils to determine the relative order of geological events. Numerical dating can then provide an estimated age for particular rocks or materials when appropriate.

Example: Fossils can show that one rock layer is younger than another. A numerical dating method may then provide an estimated age for a suitable material associated with one of those layers.

12. Quick Review

  • Fossil: preserved remains, impressions, or evidence of ancient life.
  • Relative dating: determines whether something is older or younger.
  • Numerical dating: estimates an age using numerical values.
  • Superposition: in undisturbed sedimentary layers, lower layers are generally older.
  • Cross-cutting: a feature cutting another feature is generally younger than what it cuts.
  • Index fossil: useful for comparing the relative ages of rock layers.
  • Radioactive decay: the natural transformation of unstable atoms.
  • Half-life: the time required for half of a radioactive sample to decay.
