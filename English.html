Evaluating Argumentative Texts

Learn how to identify the structure of an argument, find its main idea, recognize supporting details, and evaluate how those details support the author's central claim.

Reviewer Progress
Not completed

1. What Is an Argumentative Text?

Understand the purpose before identifying its parts.

An argumentative text is a type of writing in which an author presents a position or claim about a debatable issue and supports that position with reasons and evidence.

Important Reminder

An argument is not simply someone being angry or trying to win a fight. In academic writing, an argument is a structured position supported by reasoning and evidence.

2. Structure of an Argument

These are the major parts you should be able to identify.

Claim

The author's main position or argument. It tells the reader what the author wants them to believe or accept.

↓
Reasons

The explanations for why the author believes the claim is valid.

↓
Evidence

Facts, statistics, examples, research findings, expert opinions, or other specific information used to support the reasons.

↓
Counterclaim

A position or argument that disagrees with the author's claim.

↓
Rebuttal

The author's response to the counterclaim, explaining why the original position remains stronger.

3. Claim

The central position of the argument.

What is a claim?

A claim is a statement that expresses the position the author is arguing for. It should be something that can be supported or challenged.

✓

Arguable Claim

"Schools should provide students with more opportunities for physical activity."

×

Not Really a Claim

"The school gym has a basketball court."

4. Main Idea and Central Idea

Find what the entire argumentative text is mainly saying.

The main idea or central idea is the most important point the text communicates. In an argumentative text, the central idea is often closely connected to the author's main claim.

💡

Central Idea

What is the text mainly arguing?

→
📌

Claim

What position does the author take?

→
🔎

Support

What details prove or explain it?

5. Reasons

Reasons explain why the author makes the claim.

A reason answers the question: "Why should I believe this claim?"

Example

Schools should provide more opportunities for physical activity

because regular physical activity can help students stay active and improve their overall well-being.

6. Supporting Evidence

Evidence gives the argument something concrete to stand on.

What counts as evidence?

1

Facts

Information that can be checked or verified.

2

Statistics

Numerical information that supports a point.

3

Examples

Specific situations that illustrate the argument.

4

Expert Evidence

Information or opinions from qualified sources.

Evidence must actually support the reason.

A detail is not automatically good evidence just because it sounds convincing. It should be relevant to the point being made and should actually help support the argument.

7. Connecting Supporting Details to the Central Idea

This is one of the most important skills in this lesson.

Central Claim

Students should have regular opportunities for physical activity at school.

↓
Reason

Physical activity supports students' overall well-being.

↓
Supporting Detail

A study reports that students who regularly participate in physical activity can experience benefits associated with physical and mental well-being.

Ask yourself:

1. What is the author's main claim?
2. What reason supports that claim?
3. What evidence supports the reason?
4. How does the evidence connect back to the claim?

8. Counterclaim

Recognizing the other side of an argument.

A counterclaim is a position that challenges or disagrees with the author's main claim.

Example

Students should have more opportunities for physical activity.

Some people argue that additional physical activity could reduce the time available for academic lessons.

9. Rebuttal

Responding to the counterclaim.

A rebuttal responds to the counterclaim and explains why the author's position is still reasonable. A strong rebuttal should use reasoning or evidence rather than simply saying that the opposing side is wrong.

Example

Some people argue that additional physical activity could reduce academic learning time.

However, schools can schedule physical activity without removing essential lessons, allowing students to receive both academic instruction and opportunities for movement.

11. Worked Example

Practice identifying each part of an argument.

Sample Argument

Schools should provide students with a short reading period every school day.

Reading regularly can help students develop stronger reading habits and become more comfortable with texts.

For example, a school could set aside fifteen minutes each day for independent reading and monitor students' participation and reading progress.

Some may argue that a daily reading period would take time away from other classroom activities.

However, a short scheduled reading period could be planned as part of the school day without replacing essential academic instruction.

Identify the Parts

Part What to Find
Claim Schools should provide a short daily reading period.
Reason Regular reading can develop stronger reading habits.
Evidence A possible 15-minute daily reading program.
Counterclaim It could take time away from other activities.
Rebuttal The period could be scheduled without replacing essential instruction.

12. How to Evaluate an Argument

Don't just identify the parts. Check whether they actually work.

1

Find the Claim

What does the author want you to believe?

2

Find the Reasons

Why does the author believe the claim?

3

Check the Evidence

Is the evidence relevant and specific?

4

Check the Connection

Does the evidence actually support the reason and claim?

5

Find the Counterclaim

What might someone who disagrees say?

6

Evaluate the Rebuttal

Does the author respond logically to the opposing view?

13. Signal Words

These words can help you locate parts of an argument.

Purpose Common Signal Words
Introducing a reason because, since, one reason, first, secondly
Introducing evidence for example, for instance, according to, studies show
Introducing a counterclaim some may argue, critics say, others believe
Introducing a rebuttal however, although, nevertheless, on the other hand
Drawing a conclusion therefore, thus, in conclusion, overall

14. Don't Mix These Up

These distinctions are commonly tested.

Claim: What the author argues.
Reason: Why the author argues it.
Evidence: The specific information supporting the reason.
Counterclaim: The opposing position.
Rebuttal: The response to the opposing position.
Main/Central Idea: The most important overall point of the text.

15. Quick Review

The essentials to remember before the quiz.

Argumentative text: Presents a position supported by reasons and evidence.
Claim: The author's main position.
Reasons: Explain why the claim should be accepted.
Evidence: Specific information that supports the reasons.
Central idea: The main point of the entire text.
Counterclaim: An opposing position.
Rebuttal: A response that addresses the counterclaim.
Evaluation: Check whether the reasons and evidence actually support the claim.
