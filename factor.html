1. What Is an Exponent?

The shorthand for repeated multiplication.

An exponent (or power) tells you how many times a number, called the base, is multiplied by itself. In xⁿ, x is the base and n is the exponent.

bⁿ = b × b × b × ... (n times)

Example

2⁴ means 2 multiplied by itself 4 times: 2 × 2 × 2 × 2 = 16.

2. Laws of Exponents

Seven rules that let you simplify expressions without expanding them.

1

Product Rule: xm · xn = xm+n

2

Quotient Rule: xm ÷ xn = xm−n

3

Power Rule: (xm)n = xmn

4

Power of a Product: (xy)n = xnyn

5

Power of a Quotient: (x/y)n = xn/yn

6

Zero Exponent: x⁰ = 1 (x ≠ 0)

7

Negative Exponent: x−n = 1/xn

Worked Examples

1
x³ · x⁴ = x⁷ — add the exponents (3 + 4 = 7) because the bases match.
2
y⁷ ÷ y³ = y⁴ — subtract the exponents (7 − 3 = 4).
3
(a²)³ = a⁶ — multiply the exponents (2 × 3 = 6).
4
2⁻³ = 1/2³ = 1/8 — flip the base to make the exponent positive.

3. What Is Factoring?

Breaking an expression into the pieces that multiply back into it.

Factoring is rewriting a polynomial as a product of simpler polynomials (its factors). It's the reverse of distributing/expanding. This reviewer walks through the methods in the same order used in the Factoring Trinomials & Polynomials video linked in the Video Library below: GCF first, then grouping, then trinomials, then the special patterns.

4. Greatest Common Monomial Factor (GCF)

Always try this method first, on every problem.

Steps

1
Find the largest number that divides every term's coefficient.
2
Find the lowest power of each variable that appears in every term.
3
Divide each term by that GCF and write it outside a parenthesis.
6x³ + 9x² = 3x²(2x + 3)

One More Example

12x²y − 18xy² = 6xy(2x − 3y). The GCF of 12 and 18 is 6, and the lowest shared power of x and y across both terms is x¹ and y¹.

5. Factoring by Grouping

The go-to method for polynomials with four terms.

Steps

1
Split the polynomial into two pairs of terms.
2
Factor out the GCF from each pair separately.
3
If both pairs now share the same binomial, factor that binomial out of the whole expression.
xy + 3x + 2y + 6 = x(y + 3) + 2(y + 3) = (y + 3)(x + 2)

A Cubic Example

Grouping also works on higher-degree polynomials: 2x³ + 6x² + 5x + 15 = 2x²(x + 3) + 5(x + 3) = (x + 3)(2x² + 5).

6. Factoring Trinomials When the Leading Coefficient Is 1

x² + bx + c — the simplest trinomial case.

Steps

1
List factor pairs of c.
2
Find the pair that adds up to b.
3
Write it as (x + first)(x + second).
x² + 7x + 12 = (x + 3)(x + 4)

More Examples

x² − 2x − 15 = (x − 5)(x + 3) — check: (−5)(3) = −15 and (−5) + 3 = −2. ✓

x² − 9x + 20 = (x − 4)(x − 5) — check: (−4)(−5) = 20 and (−4) + (−5) = −9. ✓

7. Factoring Trinomials When the Leading Coefficient Is Not 1

ax² + bx + c, where a ≠ 1 — the AC method.

1
Multiply the leading coefficient a by the constant term c.
2
Find two numbers that multiply to ac and add to b.
3
Rewrite the middle term (bx) using those two numbers.
4
Finish by factoring by grouping (Method 5).
2x² + 7x + 3 = 2x² + 6x + x + 3 = 2x(x + 3) + 1(x + 3) = (x + 3)(2x + 1)

Here ac = 2 × 3 = 6. The numbers 6 and 1 multiply to 6 and add to 7, so 7x splits into 6x + x, then grouping takes over.

8. Perfect Square Trinomial

Learn to test for this pattern before trying anything else.

The 3-Part Test

1
Is the first term a perfect square?
2
Is the last term a perfect square?
3
Is the middle term equal to twice the product of the two square roots?
+

a² + 2ab + b² = (a + b)²

−

a² − 2ab + b² = (a − b)²

Testing an Example

Is 4x² + 12x + 9 a perfect square trinomial? √4x² = 2x, √9 = 3, and 2(2x)(3) = 12x — it matches the middle term, so yes: 4x² + 12x + 9 = (2x + 3)².

9. Difference of Two Squares

Spot this pattern: a squared term minus another squared term.

a² − b² = (a + b)(a − b)

Examples

x² − 25 = (x + 5)(x − 5), since 25 = 5².

4x² − 9 = (2x + 3)(2x − 3), since 4x² = (2x)² and 9 = 3².

GCF First, Then Difference of Squares

3x² − 27 = 3(x² − 9) = 3(x + 3)(x − 3). Always pull out a GCF first — it often reveals the pattern underneath.

10. Sum and Difference of Cubes

Less common, but worth memorizing the pattern.

a³ + b³ = (a + b)(a² − ab + b²)
a³ − b³ = (a − b)(a² + ab + b²)

11. Bonus: Factor Completely by Combining Patterns

Sometimes a perfect square trinomial is hiding inside a difference of squares.

Steps

1
Group three of the terms and check if they form a perfect square trinomial.
2
Rewrite that group as a squared binomial.
3
Now the whole expression is a difference of two squares — factor it that way.
x² + 6x + 9 − y² = (x + 3)² − y² = (x + 3 + y)(x + 3 − y)

The first three terms, x² + 6x + 9, factor into (x + 3)² (Method 8). That turns the whole expression into (x + 3)² − y², a difference of squares (Method 9), which factors into two binomials.

12. Quick Comparison: Factoring Patterns

Which pattern applies? Check the shape of the expression first.

13. Quick Review

The most important things to remember before a quiz.

Same base, multiplying: add the exponents.
Same base, dividing: subtract the exponents.
Power of a power: multiply the exponents.
Anything to the 0: equals 1.
Negative exponent: flip it to the other side of the fraction.
Factoring order to try: GCF → grouping (4 terms) → trinomial methods → special patterns (squares/cubes).
Trinomial (a = 1): find two numbers that multiply to c and add to b.
Trinomial (a ≠ 1): use the AC method, then finish by grouping.
Perfect square trinomial test: both ends are perfect squares, and the middle is twice their product.
a² − b²: difference of squares → (a+b)(a−b).
Factor completely: a perfect square trinomial can hide inside a bigger difference of squares — group it first.
